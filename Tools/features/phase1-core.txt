# High Water Mark - phase 1: the core (spec section SX 10, "What to build first")
# Run:  .\claude-batch.ps1 -Features Tools\features\phase1-core.txt -PlanOnly
# The full design is in game-spec.txt at the repo root; each feature names the spec section to read.
# Lines starting '#' are ignored. A feature runs until the next "N." line, so never start a
# continuation line with a number followed by '.' or ')'.

1. Project foundation. The repo has no code yet. Set up a first-person 3D browser game in TypeScript with Vite
   and Three.js (WebGL), with Vitest for unit tests and npm scripts "dev", "build" and "test". The game is played
   in first person throughout; the only 2D screens are the paper map, the pack and menus.
   Lay out folders for: world data (terrain, generation), simulation (time, walking, survival), UI (paper map,
   HUD) and rendering. Keep simulation code free of Three.js so it can be unit-tested headless.
   There is no world seed and no per-play variation for now: there is one fixed world, generated once, offline,
   by a Node build script that you write and run yourself, and committed as data. The game only loads that data at
   runtime and never rolls dice for the world. Put the build scripts under tools/worldgen and the committed output
   in the world data folder. A small deterministic RNG (e.g. mulberry32) may be used inside the build scripts only.
   Add a CLAUDE.md describing the stack, folder layout and how to build and test, so later sessions don't survey.

2. Real terrain for a 10 km x 10 km patch around Okehampton (spec SX 02, "Three levels of detail").
   The patch must include the start point at the old target railway below Rowtor (50.7024 N, 3.9942 W, about 470 m)
   plus Yes Tor (619 m), High Willhays, the West Okement valley, Okehampton Camp and Okehampton town.
   Write an offline Node script that turns free OS Terrain 50 height data (and OS OpenMap Local or OpenStreetMap
   for roads, rail, rivers, woodland and buildings with their type) into a compact game data file on a 50 m grid,
   and commit only the processed output, not the raw downloads. If the data can't be downloaded in the session,
   document the exact download steps and ship a clearly-labelled synthetic stand-in in the same format so later
   work isn't blocked. Never randomise terrain: hills, rivers, roads and towns are real.

3. High-water mark and flood bands (spec SX 03). Store one high-water value per 1 km tile: a smooth slope from
   about 430 m on Dartmoor (south-west) falling to about 280 m at Mendip (north-east), plus gentle variation,
   computed once by the world build script and committed (no per-play shift). Classify every 50 m cell into a band: above the mark,
   strandline (top 20 m below the mark), scour zone (deeper), or still under water. Run a "fill the hollows"
   (priority-flood depression filling) pass once at world creation to find how deep each closed hollow holds
   water, so trapped lakes can later fall a few centimetres a day. Run it in the build script and commit the
   result. Unit-test the band classification and the fill.

4. First-person world view. Render the patch in 3D from eye height: a chunked terrain mesh built from the real
   heights (interpolated between the 50 m posts, with fixed small-scale roughness for close ground,
   the same on every visit), with level of
   detail so distant hills stay visible out to at least 8 km, since the view from tors is central to the game.
   Sky, sun and a light fog; ground coloured so the flood bands read by eye (scoured ground stripped and mud-grey,
   a strandline wrack line, standing water as a flat water surface). Roads, rivers, woods and buildings as simple
   low-poly shapes. Mouse-look with pointer lock.

5. Overhead debug view (spec SX 10, first item: "viewed from above"). A developer-only toggle that shows the patch
   from above with height shading, the flood bands, the 1 km tile grid, the high-water value per tile and the
   player's position and facing. It is a testing tool, not part of play.

6. Game clock and walking with a load model (spec SX 05). A game clock (start Day 0, 18:00, in July) driving the
   sun position and day/night lighting in the first-person view. First-person movement (WASD) following the terrain,
   with walking speed from ground type, slope, load and party. Load bands as a share of each person's body weight: up to 20% normal pace; 20 to 30% about 15%
   slower and tire faster; 30 to 40% about 35% slower plus ankle/knee injury risk climbing rough ground; over 40%
   short hops and frequent rests. Work it out separately for parent and child (a 12-year-old of 40 kg hits 20% at
   8 kg); the party moves at the slower one's pace. Model a pack with item weights (camping kit about 5 to 6 kg
   each, tin of food 0.4 kg, water 1 kg per litre, gas canister 0.4 kg). Targets: 15 to 25 km a day on paths,
   5 to 10 km through debris. Unit-test the pace maths.

7. Paper map with pencil (spec SX 04). A separate OS-style paper map screen showing only the pre-disaster world:
   contours, roads, rivers, towns, grid squares. It shows none of the damage and the game never draws on it.
   The player draws routes with a pencil tool, adds text notes, crosses things out and erases. Marks persist in
   the save. Optional light hint mode (off by default) that adds only a faint "you saw something here" mark.

8. Planned versus real leg times (spec SX 04). When the player marks a leg on the paper map, show a planned time
   using Naismith's rule (5 km an hour plus an hour for every 600 m of climbing, from the real height data),
   slowed for load and party. After walking the leg, show the real time beside it. Unit-test the Naismith maths.

9. Fixed district data (spec SX 02). Generate every 1 km tile's hidden district data once, in the world build
   script, and commit it; the game loads it and keeps it hidden from the player: local flood height, large craters,
   which bridges stand,
   which roads are blocked, fires, gas hollows, and counts of animals. Place by likelihood, not evenly:
   a bridge 40 m below the mark falls about 9 times in 10 and one above it about 1 in 10; a road through a wood
   below the mark is nearly always blocked, an open moor road above it nearly never. Also decide, once, which
   side of the moor took the worst of the storm and which two or three major bridges survived. Check the result
   yourself in the overhead debug view and hand-fix anything implausible before committing. Unit-test that the
   committed data loads and matches its schema, and that bridge and road states follow the likelihood rules overall.

10. Fixed ground detail (spec SX 02). Generate each 1 km tile's 2 to 5 m ground detail once in the world build
   script and commit it as compact per-tile files, loaded when the player enters the tile (or views it through the
   scope): rubble, fallen trees, small craters, wrack piles
   and loot per building (using building type). Below the mark, debris density depends on distance from roads and
   settlements (thick near towns, thin across open fields) and piles up in valley bottoms, against stone walls and
   against standing Devon hedge banks. Crater sizes follow a steep curve: for each 50 m crater about ten of 15 m and
   about a hundred of 5 m. Store detail sparsely (object lists, not full cell grids) so the committed files stay
   small. Save only what the player changed (items
   taken, doors forced, carcasses), never the whole tile. Show the detail in the first-person view as simple
   low-poly instanced objects (rubble, trunks, cars, wrack, crater rims) and carve craters into the terrain mesh,
   with nearby tiles detailed and distant ones left at district level so performance holds.

11. Damage blockers with costs and ways past (spec SX 02, "Damage blocks"). Each blocker is a choice, not a wall:
   pocked ground slows walking about 10% and trips you in the dark; craters 5 to 50 m to go round, large ones with a
   ring of thrown-out rock and sometimes standing water; rare strike zones of 100 m and up (2 to 5 in the patch), burning
   or glassy, visible from tors as smoke; wrack dams at bridges and valley narrows (climb across slowly with injury
   risk, or go upstream); bridges out (ford, swim losing unsealed gear, or detour); landslips on steep valley sides;
   fallen woodland (very slow); silt and mud flats (slow, tiring, soft patches can trap you); gas hollows on valley
   floors days 1 to 5 only (warned by dead birds and no insects); standing water (wade, raft, or go round).
   Wire their movement costs into the walking model.

12. Always leave a way through (spec SX 02). In the world build script, after generating the districts, check that
   a walkable route exists from the start to the far edge of the playable area (later: to Priddy), and reopen one
   blocked crossing if it doesn't, before the data is committed. Make the check a reusable function, unit-tested on
   a small hand-made district grid that would otherwise be blocked, plus a test that the committed world passes.

13. The scope from high ground (spec SX 04). Raising binoculars or a rifle scope gives a zoomed first-person view
   (narrow field of view, scope or binocular overlay). From a tor or hilltop it reveals the district-level damage on
   every tile in line of sight from the real height data, up to about 8 km in clear weather and less in rain or
   smoke: distant tiles that were drawn plain now show their craters, smoke, broken bridges and blocked roads.
   Nothing is added to the paper map; the player marks it themselves. Climbing to look should cost real time and energy.
